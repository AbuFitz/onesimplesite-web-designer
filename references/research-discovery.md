# Evidence-led website research

Use this reference when the brief depends on facts outside the supplied materials: market context, competitors, local intent, category conventions, audience language, integrations, current pricing or regulation, or visual references.

## Frame the decision

Research only what can change the site. Start with a short question set:

- What decision will this evidence support?
- Which claims could become public copy?
- What is time-sensitive or jurisdiction-specific?
- Which source would be authoritative?
- What can remain an explicit assumption?

Split work into four lanes as relevant:

1. **Business truth:** offer, service area, people, proof, process, policies, contact routes.
2. **Audience reality:** search language, urgent questions, objections, accessibility and device context.
3. **Market landscape:** direct alternatives, category expectations, whitespace, visual sameness, conversion patterns.
4. **Delivery constraints:** platform, integration, legal, privacy, performance, content ownership, maintenance.

## Source standard

Prefer primary sources: the client's materials, official registers, platform documentation, standards bodies, first-party product documentation, and direct competitor sites. Use independent reporting or reviews to understand perception, not as proof of a client's claim.

For consequential claims:

- corroborate with two sources when practical;
- record the URL, publisher, publication or update date, and access date;
- distinguish direct evidence from inference;
- note conflicts instead of averaging them away;
- never convert an absence of evidence into a positive claim.

Search results and AI summaries are leads, not sources. Open the supporting page. Do not quote or imitate competitor copy.

## Competitive review

Inspect a small representative set rather than collecting dozens of screenshots. Record observable patterns across:

- promise and CTA;
- information order;
- evidence and trust;
- pricing or enquiry posture;
- type, color, density, imagery, geometry, and motion;
- mobile behavior and accessibility;
- technical weight and obvious failures.

Extract category conventions that users rely on, then identify one or two areas where originality helps. Differentiation should come from the client's actual position, not arbitrary visual weirdness.

## Reference-site translation

For each reference, write what is useful in concrete terms: editorial scale contrast, compressed navigation, warm flash photography, hard horizontal rules, camera easing, or a specific content rhythm. Also record what must not be copied: identity, proprietary assets, wording, distinctive illustrations, and near-identical composition.

## Research output

Keep a compact ledger:

| Item | Status | Evidence | Site consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Service area | verified | supplied page / official profile | location in title, hero, contact |
| Review claim | provisional | third-party listing only | do not quote until approved |
| Audience prefers calls | inferred | category pattern | keep phone prominent; test with client |
| Booking system | blocking | no account or destination | use labelled placeholder; do not claim live |

End with decisions, remaining blockers, and the content/assets still needed. Do not bury uncertainty in prose.

## Anti-hallucination gate

Before build and before delivery, scan every visible proper noun, number, date, place, testimonial, credential, client logo, price, guarantee, response time, result, and superlative. Each must map to the ledger or be visibly labelled as sample/provisional. If it does not, remove or neutralise it.
